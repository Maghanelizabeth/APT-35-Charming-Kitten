Charming Kitten (APT35) – Recent Activity Targeting Energy Sector & Critical Infrastructure

Executive Summary

Charming Kitten (APT35) is a nation-state cyber espionage group linked to Iran’s Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), increasingly active against the Energy sector and other critical infrastructure over the last 12–24 months . In recent operations, APT35 has employed highly sophisticated social engineering tactics – including long-term reconnaissance, personalized spear-phishing (email and social media lures), and even real-time phishing via video call invitations – to compromise target organizations and individuals   . These tailored attacks often lead to initial network access and credential theft, enabling the group to infiltrate both corporate IT environments and personal accounts of key employees. Once inside, APT35 deploys custom malware and stealthy techniques to achieve persistent access, move laterally, and siphon sensitive data over extended periods. Recent intelligence leaks have shed light on APT35’s inner workings, revealing a military-style structure behind their campaigns, complete with dedicated teams and measured performance quotas . This report outlines APT35’s tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) as observed in the past two years, with emphasis on Energy sector targeting and the threat this group poses to critical infrastructure.


Threat Actor Overview and IRGC Affiliation

Charming Kitten (APT35) is an Iranian state-sponsored advanced persistent threat group active since at least 2014 and is primarily focused on cyber-espionage . Also known by aliases such as Phosphorus and Mint Sandstorm, APT35 has historically targeted government officials, military personnel, diplomats, journalists, and academics, aligning its operations with Iranian national security and foreign policy priorities   .
Recent intelligence has firmly tied APT35 to the IRGC, specifically the IRGC’s Intelligence Organization (IRGC-IO) . In late 2025, a cache of leaked internal documents confirmed that APT35 functions as a highly organized, bureaucratic cyber warfare unit rather than a loose hacker collective . These leaks revealed that APT35 operates with a formal chain-of-command, unit rosters, and even performance reviews – effectively mirroring a military hierarchy in cyberspace   . For example, the group’s leadership structure includes coordinators and supervisors overseeing specialized sub-teams (for exploit development, phishing, malware operation, etc.), with each operator’s work tracked by monthly reports and quotas   . This militarized operational style provides APT35 with disciplined, repeatable processes to conduct large-scale and persistent intelligence collection campaigns.
Motivations: APT35 is assessed to be working on behalf of the Iranian state to advance strategic interests. Their goals center on stealing sensitive information (such as energy sector strategies, geopolitical plans, and intellectual property), conducting surveillance on high-profile individuals, and potentially preparing the battlespace for future operations . While financial gain is typically not the primary driver (aside from a few outlier incidents), the group’s activities serve Iran’s intelligence and geopolitical objectives, including monitoring adversaries, gathering diplomatic or economic leverage, and securing insight into critical infrastructure that could be vital in times of conflict   .
Recent Activity Targeting the Energy Sector & Critical Infrastructure
Over the past two years, Charming Kitten has aggressively expanded its operations against energy and other critical infrastructure sectors. The leaked IRGC-IO documents and various threat intelligence reports show energy companies and critical infrastructure operators listed among APT35’s targets   . Notably, energy sector entities in the Middle East (e.g., in Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Kuwait) and parts of Asia have been targeted, in addition to the group’s ongoing campaigns against government and telecom organizations. By infiltrating these industries, APT35 aims to collect intelligence on energy production, policy decisions, and infrastructure designs – information that can provide Iran with economic and strategic advantages   .
APT35’s interest in critical infrastructure is primarily espionage-driven. There have been no confirmed destructive attacks on industrial control systems by Charming Kitten; instead, the group focuses on quietly penetrating networks and stealing data. For example, an analysis of a 2024 APT35 operation revealed extensive data exfiltration (over 74 GB from one victim) and “Active Directory” domination, rather than any immediate disruptive action. However, the presence of an adversary with nation-state backing inside an energy company’s network is itself a serious risk – they could potentially use that access for sabotage if geopolitical circumstances changed. Indeed, security experts warn that APT35 likely possesses the capability for disruptive attacks on critical infrastructure, even if they have so far mostly exercised espionage and surveillance .
In recent energy-sector related cases, APT35 has demonstrated patient, multi-step operations. The group may begin by compromising peripheral organizations or suppliers (a supply-chain attack) to indirectly reach a target utility or energy firm . They have also been linked to campaigns against telecom and satellite providers (often considered part of critical national infrastructure), highlighting the breadth of their focus on systems that underpin both economic and national security   .
Example Incident (2025): According to threat intelligence disclosures, APT35 conducted espionage campaigns that compromised multiple Middle Eastern energy companies through a combination of phishing and exploiting unpatched internet-facing systems   . The actors harvested corporate emails, internal documents, and account credentials from these victims, likely seeking insights into oil and gas production, energy infrastructure projects, and government energy policies. In one instance, Saudi energy organizations were targeted as part of a broader campaign, with stolen data feeding into IRGC analyses of Gulf energy policy and regional power dynamics . These activities underline APT35’s role in gathering economic and policy intelligence related to critical infrastructure for Iran.

Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs)

APT35 has continually refined its TTPs to improve the success of its operations. The past two years have seen the group employing a blend of traditional cyber-espionage techniques and novel social engineering tricks, making them a formidable threat to even well-defended organizations:

•	Initial Access – Spear Phishing & Social Engineering: 
Charming Kitten’s primary entry vector is spear-phishing – sending targeted phishing emails or messages to carefully chosen individuals. What sets APT35 apart is the degree of customization and pre-attack reconnaissance involved. Operators often spend weeks studying their targets’ online presence and social networks, then craft highly convincing lures (e.g. personalized emails, conference invites, or partner organization messages) tailored to each victim   . The attackers use fake personas on platforms like LinkedIn and WhatsApp – for instance, pretending to be recruiters, journalists, or colleagues – to engage targets in conversation and build trust before delivering a malicious link or attachment   . In some cases, they compromise a real person’s email account first, then use it to email targets making the phishing email appear genuinely internal or from a known associate . This patient, human-focused approach often tricks even savvy victims into clicking links or providing credentials.

•	Evolving Phishing Tactics (Real-Time Credential Theft): 
Charming Kitten’s social engineering has grown more audacious. In 2025, security teams discovered APT35 impersonating video conferencing services as a phishing lure . The attackers send realistic meeting invitations or schedule calls via fake Zoom/Skype/Google Meet links. When the target attempts to join the meeting, they are presented with a fraudulent login page where any credentials (and two-factor authentication codes) entered are captured live by the attackers . This allows APT35 to breach email or VPN accounts immediately, even defeating many standard two-factor authentication safeguards. In essence, APT35 can execute man-in-the-middle attacks during live social interactions, exploiting the urgency and trust of a scheduled call to steal login data on the spot. This tactic has been used against executives, researchers, and officials, and could easily be adapted to high-level personnel in energy firms or government energy agencies.

•	Exploitation of Public-Facing Applications: 
While social engineering is the preferred door-opener, APT35 also targets internet-facing systems with known vulnerabilities. They have rapidly adopted exploits for critical flaws – for example, they were observed quickly weaponizing Microsoft Exchange server exploits (such as ProxyShell in 2021 and a 2024 Exchange CVE-2024-1709) to breach organizational email servers and steal contact lists and emails   . The group’s playbooks, revealed in the 2025 leak, detail how they scan for vulnerable servers and use those exploits (including Ivanti software RCEs and Exchange Autodiscover flaws) to gain access, install web shells for persistence, and extract Global Address Lists and credentials from mail systems . By compromising email infrastructure or other critical servers, APT35 can bypass the need for phishing in some cases, directly obtaining valid accounts and sensitive communications. They have also been known to leverage third-party compromises (supply chain attacks) – for instance, breaching a trusted vendor or government partner’s network – as another route into a target organization’s critical systems .

•	Use of Custom Malware and Tools: 
APT35 uses a range of malware, from publicly available tools to their own bespoke creations. In late 2024, they were discovered using a new custom remote access trojan (RAT) called BellaCiao to enhance their foothold in victim networks . Researchers have documented PowerShell-based backdoors like CharmPower (a.k.a. PowerStar) and PowerLess that are unique to this group . These malware families enable keystroke logging, data theft, and remote control of infected machines, while blending into normal processes to avoid detection . APT35 is also adept at modifying or disabling security tools and using fileless techniques to remain stealthy within victims’ networks   . The group continually updates its toolset – for example, introducing new malware variants or obfuscation methods – to stay ahead of defenses and ensure they can maintain persistence.

•	Credential Theft & Lateral Movement: 
Gaining valid user credentials is a central objective for APT35. Through phishing and post-exploitation techniques, the group harvests credentials on a large scale . They dump password hashes and clear-text passwords from memory using tools like Mimikatz, exploit additional systems via stolen VPN/Single Sign-On tokens, and abuse legitimate admin tools (PowerShell, WMI, remote desktop) to move laterally through networks. APT35’s leaked reports reference a “credential and access” team dedicated to extracting passwords (e.g., via LSASS dumping) and reusing tokens for broader access   . By accumulating administrator and user credentials – including those of personal email or cloud accounts – the attackers pivot across both IT and OT environments, potentially granting visibility into industrial control systems if those networks are connected. This methodical lateral movement allows escalation to key servers like domain controllers (Active Directory), giving APT35 control over large swaths of an organization’s infrastructure .

•	Data Exfiltration and Persistence: 
Once APT35 has established itself within a network, it typically remains active for an extended duration to maximize intelligence collection. The group is known for long-term persistence rather than quick smash-and-grab operations   . Stolen data (emails, documents, databases) is quietly funneled out using encrypted channels or cloud services to evade detection . For example, in one case APT35 maintained continuous data exfiltration – over 74 GB from a single target – by stealthily transferring archives to cloud storage over weeks. The attackers also keep access “warm” by monitoring compromised email accounts in real time (sometimes termed **“Real-Time Monitoring” teams) and using stolen contact lists to fuel new phishing waves (their so-called “HERV” phishing cycle)   . This cyclical approach means that once APT35 penetrates an energy organization or its partners, they can recursively find new victims and maintain a presence indefinitely, unless detected and eradicated.

•	Targets and Impact on Energy & Critical Infrastructure: 
While APT35’s campaigns are global, many of their recent targets are in strategically important sectors and regions. The Energy sector has featured prominently – both directly and via related industries like petrochemicals, utilities, and government energy agencies   . For instance, the group’s 2024–2025 campaigns targeted energy firms and ministries in the Middle East (including Gulf states) and Asia as part of broader espionage efforts   . These intrusions have likely yielded valuable insights into oil and gas production, national energy policies, and critical infrastructure projects, information that can bolster Iran’s strategic position regionally . Additionally, telecommunications and technology providers – essential components of modern critical infrastructure – have been breached by APT35 or closely affiliated groups, as seen in a major early-2024 spear-phishing operation against global satellite and telecom firms (dubbed “Subtle Snail”) associated with Charming Kitten’s tactics   . The impact of APT35’s activities on the energy sector is primarily the loss of sensitive data and credentials, which could facilitate intellectual property theft, espionage on energy negotiations, or even pre-positioning in case of future conflicts. The long-term presence of an IRGC-linked adversary in critical networks also raises concerns about potential disruptive actions, even if none have been confirmed to date .

Conclusion

In summary, Charming Kitten (APT35) represents one of the most persistent and dangerous cyber threats to the Energy sector and other critical infrastructure, particularly over the last two years. Backed by the resources and strategic direction of Iran’s security apparatus, APT35 has blended patient, human-centered social engineering with advanced technical exploits to penetrate organizations deeply and harvest sensitive data. Their willingness to target individuals’ personal lives and use creative lures like video call invitations demonstrates a determined, espionage-focused mindset. At the same time, the revelations of APT35’s military-style organization under the IRGC highlight that we are dealing with an adversary that operates with discipline, clear objectives, and ample government support   .
For energy companies and critical infrastructure operators, the risk is elevated. APT35’s campaigns have shown that no vector is off-limits – from spear-phishing employees and suppliers, to exploiting unpatched enterprise software and cloud platforms   . Defenders in the Energy sector should remain vigilant, ensuring robust phishing defenses, up-to-date patch management, multi-factor authentication with resistance to phishing, and proactive threat hunting for signs of this group’s known tactics (e.g., unfamiliar webmail logins, suspicious Exchange server activity, and the presence of custom APT35 malware or web shells). By understanding Charming Kitten’s recent exploits and adaptable TTPs, security teams can better anticipate and counter this state-sponsored threat before it gains a foothold in critical systems.
